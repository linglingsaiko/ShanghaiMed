Three years of desk work. Three years of ibuprofen. Three years of waking up stiff, sitting through meetings with a dull ache radiating from his lower back, and spacing out at his Manhattan office because the pain was louder than the spreadsheet.

His doctor suggested an epidural steroid injection. The quote made him pause. Physical therapy sessions ran $150–$300 out of pocket in New York, and the relief was temporary.

He was planning a trip to Shanghai anyway — the Bund, the food, the whole experience. Two weeks before his flight, he messaged our team:

"I heard acupuncture in China is the real deal. My colleague swears it fixed his back. I think that's crazy. But… I can barely sleep some nights. What do you think?"

## What we told him

Shanghai is home to some of the most experienced acupuncture practitioners in the world. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) isn't "alternative" here — it's integrated into the mainstream hospital system, regulated by the same health authorities, and practised by licensed physicians who train for years at institutions like Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, one of China's foremost TCM universities.

And the cost? A fraction of what he'd pay in New York.

"Do I need a medical visa?" he asked.

No. Acupuncture is an outpatient procedure — no hospitalisation, no special paperwork. He'd already sorted his 10-year multi-entry tourist visa before the trip, the standard arrangement for Americans visiting China.

He booked the same week.

## Day 2: First session

He landed at Pudong on a Thursday evening. Our team met him at arrivals and drove him to a hotel near a TCM clinic in Xuhui district.

Next morning, we walked in together. The clinic was clean and calm — not the crowded, chaotic image he'd imagined. The practitioner, a TCM physician with over 20 years of experience, reviewed his symptoms: chronic lower back pain, worse after sitting, occasional numbness radiating down his left leg.

The diagnosis, in TCM terms, pointed to stagnation along the Bladder meridian — a common pattern for chronic lower back pain from prolonged sitting. The treatment plan: acupuncture at 6–8 points per session, plus cupping therapy. Three sessions over five days for noticeable improvement.

The price: 280 RMB per session — about $40 USD.

"Forty dollars?" he said. "My last physical therapy session in New York ran nearly $200 out of pocket."

The practitioner inserted thin, hair-like needles at specific points along his lower back, hips, and legs. He barely felt them go in.

"You may feel a dull ache or heaviness," the practitioner said. "That means the Qi is moving."

He lay there for 30 minutes. He dozed off.

After the needles came out, the practitioner did cupping — glass cups placed on his back, creating gentle suction to improve blood flow. The circular marks would fade in about a week.

He stood up. Stretched.

"Huh. It feels… looser. Like someone untied a knot I didn't know was there."

## Day 4: Second session

By the second session, he was already a convert.

"I slept through the night last night. First time in months."

The practitioner adjusted the needle points — less focus on the hips, more on the lumbar region. Added moxibustion: dried mugwort warmed near the skin to promote deeper circulation.

"This smells like a campfire," he said. "I love it."

## Day 6: Third session and the Bund

Final session in the morning. He reported significant improvement in pain levels. No ibuprofen in four days.

Afternoon: we walked the Bund at sunset. He stood at the railing, looking across the Huangpu River at the Lujiazui skyline.

"I came to Shanghai for the architecture. I'm leaving with a working lower back."

Dinner at a local Shanghainese restaurant — red-braised pork, xiaolongbao, stir-fried river shrimp. He ate everything without wincing.

## What the science says

Acupuncture isn't folklore. The largest analysis to date — 39 clinical trials, 20,827 patients — found that acupuncture provides real pain relief for chronic pain conditions including back pain, beyond a placebo effect. The treatment effect decreased by only about 15% one year after treatment ended. ([Vickers et al., *Journal of Pain*, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29198932/))

In 2019, the World Health Organization included traditional medicine diagnoses in ICD-11, the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases — a milestone in TCM's integration into global health frameworks.

## Honest tips before you try TCM in Shanghai

- **Choose licensed practitioners at public hospitals or established TCM clinics** — not random street shops. In China, acupuncturists must hold TCM physician licences.
- **Bring previous medical records** if you have them — imaging, diagnoses, medication history. A good practitioner will spend 15–20 minutes understanding your full history before needling.
- **Plan 3–5 sessions for chronic conditions.** One session won't fix years of pain. TCM works cumulatively.
- **Combine with your travel itinerary.** Most clinics offer morning and afternoon slots. You can sightsee the same day.
- **Don't expect miracles from one session.** But don't be surprised if you feel something shift after the first.
