The first time a Western patient steps into a TCM consultation room, parts of the experience can feel unfamiliar.

Alongside questions about your main symptoms and medical history, the doctor may place three fingers on your wrist, look carefully at your tongue, and ask about sleep, appetite, digestion, energy and temperature — details that can seem unrelated to the problem you came in for. In a hospital setting, the doctor may also review blood tests, imaging or other records, and order further investigations when needed.

A common first reaction is: "She described how I'd been feeling before I explained it properly." Some patients find this impressive; others are skeptical. Most want to understand what the doctor is actually doing.

Here is what typically happens in a standard TCM consultation, and why it works the way it does.

## What to expect: the four examinations

A TCM physician will ask about your main concern and relevant history. The difference is that these questions are integrated with observation, pulse taking and other findings rather than treated as a standalone checklist. The exact order varies by doctor and situation; urgent or red-flag symptoms should always be addressed first.

The diagnostic process in TCM is built around what are called the Four Examinations (四诊):

**1. Looking (望 wàng).** The doctor observes you from the moment you walk in: your posture, your complexion, the brightness of your eyes, the colour and coating of your tongue. Tongue diagnosis is a detailed art in itself — the tongue is treated as a microsystem reflecting the state of your internal organs, with different areas corresponding to different organ systems. A trained TCM physician reads tongue body colour, coating thickness, coating colour, shape, moisture level, and even the presence of teeth marks or cracks.

**2. Listening and smelling (闻 wén).** This includes listening to the quality of your voice, your breathing, any cough, and — less frequently discussed — the smell of your breath or body odour, which can indicate specific patterns in TCM theory. In practice, most modern TCM physicians rely more on the other three examinations.

**3. Asking (问 wèn).** The physician asks about your main concern, symptom onset and course, previous diagnoses and treatment, medications, and relevant medical history. They will usually also ask targeted questions that help with TCM pattern differentiation: How is your sleep? Your appetite? Your digestion? Are your hands and feet cold or warm? How is your energy through the day? For women, questions about the menstrual cycle may be relevant. These details help confirm or rule out a specific pattern (证 zhèng); they do not replace a standard medical history.

**4. Palpating (切 qiè).** This includes pulse diagnosis — taking the radial pulse on both wrists — and may include palpating affected areas of the body. Pulse diagnosis is the part that Western patients often find most unfamiliar. The physician places three fingers at three positions on each wrist (cun, guan, chi — 寸关尺) and uses different pressure levels. Classical TCM pulse diagnosis describes 28 pulse qualities, including floating, deep, slow, rapid, slippery, wiry and thready; in an individual consultation, the physician assesses the features relevant to that patient rather than mechanically reporting all 28.

## The moment that surprises every first-time patient

Here is the part that produces the "she just knew" reaction:

The doctor takes your pulse for one to three minutes, looks at your tongue, and then starts telling you your own symptoms — not asking, **telling**.

"Trouble sleeping?"
"Your appetite's been poor?"
"Cold hands and feet?"
"You feel tired after eating?"

And you nod. Every single one.

This isn't magic, and it isn't cold reading. It's pattern recognition. The combination of your pulse qualities, tongue presentation, facial complexion, and the visible signs the doctor observed as you walked in points to a specific pattern — for example, Heart-Spleen deficiency (心脾两虚), a common pattern in overworked, stressed patients that presents with insomnia, poor appetite, fatigue, and cold extremities. Once the pattern is identified, the associated symptoms cluster predictably.

What feels like mind-reading to a patient is, from the physician's perspective, the equivalent of a cardiologist looking at an EKG and telling you what symptoms to expect before you describe them.

## What happens after the diagnosis

Once the pattern is identified, the treatment plan is selected — and it is almost always a combination of modalities, not a single intervention. A typical first visit for a chronic issue might include several of the following:

### Acupuncture

Very thin, sterile, single-use needles (about the thickness of a human hair) are inserted at specific acupuncture points (穴位 xuéwèi). The selection of points is determined by the identified pattern — not just by where your pain is. For Heart-Spleen deficiency with insomnia, for example, a physician may needle Shenmen (HT7, at the wrist crease) for calming the spirit, Neiguan (PC6, on the forearm) for harmonising the chest and calming, and Hegu (LI4, on the hand) for regulating the overall flow of qi.

Most patients feel a slight pinch or a dull ache when the needle goes in, followed by a heavy or tingling sensation — not pain. Many people find the experience deeply relaxing and fall asleep during the 20–30 minute retention period.

### Ear seeds (耳穴压豆)

Small seeds — typically from the Vaccaria plant (王不留行, wáng bù liú xíng) — attached to tiny adhesive plasters are placed on specific points on the outer ear. The ear is treated as a microsystem reflecting the entire body in TCM theory, and ear seeds act as a form of sustained acupressure between sessions. The patient presses the seeds several times a day to maintain stimulation. They stay on for 3–5 days, then fall off naturally or are removed.

For insomnia and digestive issues, common points include Shenmen (神门, calms the spirit), Heart (心, nourishes heart qi), and Stomach/Spleen (胃/脾, supports digestion).

### Herbal formula

If herbs are prescribed, the physician writes a custom formula based on the identified pattern — not a standard product off the shelf. This is one of the fundamental differences between seeing a licensed TCM physician and buying TCM-style supplements online.

Formulas are structured around a classical hierarchy called the sovereign-minister-assistant-courier (君臣佐使) principle: one or two chief herbs address the main pattern; deputy herbs reinforce the chief or address co-occurring patterns; assistant herbs moderate harsh effects or treat secondary symptoms; courier herbs guide the formula to specific channels or harmonise the whole.

For Heart-Spleen deficiency, a classical base formula called Gui Pi Tang (归脾汤, "Restore the Spleen Decoction") is often used as the starting point, then adjusted — a herb added, a herb removed, dosages changed — based on your individual presentation. The formula is adjusted again at follow-up visits as your condition changes.

This is what TCM physicians mean when they say they treat the person, not the disease: two patients with the same Western diagnosis may receive completely different formulas if their patterns differ; two patients with different Western diagnoses may receive similar formulas if their patterns are the same.

### Tuina (medical massage), cupping, or moxibustion

Depending on your condition, the physician may also include tuina (Chinese medical massage, different from Western relaxation massage — it works along the same meridians as acupuncture), cupping (glass or silicone cups creating suction on the skin to promote circulation and release muscle tension), or moxibustion (burning dried mugwort near specific points to warm and stimulate).

## How long does a first visit take?

A first TCM consultation at a special-needs clinic or international patient clinic of a Shanghai public hospital typically takes 20–40 minutes — longer than a standard 3–5 minute public clinic visit, though shorter than many Western specialist appointments. This is one of the main reasons we book international patients into these clinics rather than the general public queue. Follow-up visits are shorter, usually 10–20 minutes plus treatment time.

Acupuncture sessions themselves are usually 20–30 minutes of needle retention time. The full visit, including consultation, acupuncture, and any other modalities, typically runs 60–90 minutes for a first visit, 45–60 minutes for follow-ups.

## What TCM is good for — and what it isn't

This is where honesty matters more than anything else.

People commonly seek TCM for chronic musculoskeletal pain, migraine prevention, sleep problems, digestive symptoms, menstrual or menopausal concerns, and allergy symptoms. The evidence is not equally strong across these conditions. For example, some clinical guidelines support acupuncture as an option for chronic low back pain, and systematic reviews support acupuncture for migraine prevention; for many other uses, evidence is limited, mixed, or still emerging. TCM should be considered condition by condition and is often used alongside Western care rather than as an automatic replacement for it.

There are situations where TCM is not the right first stop. Acute chest pain, signs of stroke, severe trauma, progressive neurological symptoms, suspected cancer, high fever in children — these all require immediate Western medical evaluation. A good TCM physician will tell you this directly and refer you to the appropriate department.

The best TCM physicians I've worked with don't compete with Western medicine; they read your MRI reports, check your lab results, ask what medications you're on, and tell you when what you need is not a needle or a herb but a trip to a specialist in another department.

## How to prepare for your first visit

If you're planning to try TCM in Shanghai, here are practical things to do before your appointment:

- **Bring your medical records.** Imaging reports, lab results, medication lists, previous diagnoses — a good TCM physician will want to see them, even if they'll also do their own diagnosis.
- **Don't scrape your tongue or brush your tongue coating** the morning of the visit — the tongue coating is part of the diagnosis.
- **Avoid drinking coffee, eating strongly coloured foods, or smoking** for at least an hour before the visit, as these can temporarily change your tongue appearance and pulse quality.
- **Wear loose clothing** that can be rolled up to your elbows and knees — many acupuncture points are on the forearms and lower legs.
- **Don't come on a completely empty stomach** or immediately after a large meal.
- **Write down your symptoms** even though the doctor may not start by asking — having them ready helps when the doctor asks confirmatory questions.
- **Bring a translator** if you don't speak Mandarin. Nuance matters enormously in TCM consultations, and Google Translate will not help you explain "dull pain that moves around in a way that's worse after eating cold food."
