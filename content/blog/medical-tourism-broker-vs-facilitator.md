# Medical Tourism Broker vs. Facilitator: What's the Difference and Why It Matters

Two patients landed at Shanghai Pudong International Airport within a week of each other last year. Both needed the same orthopedic procedure. Both had found a company online that promised to "handle everything" for their medical trip to China.

The first patient was met at arrivals by his coordinator. His medical records had already been translated and filed with the hospital. His pre-op consultations were scheduled for the next morning. When post-operative swelling worried him at 2 a.m. three days later, he messaged his coordinator — and got an answer within minutes.

The second patient received a driver, a hotel voucher, and a hospital appointment. When he developed a fever on day four, he called the company that had arranged his trip. He was told to "contact the hospital directly." The company had already collected its fee — not from him, but from the hospital he was now struggling to communicate with.

Same city. Same procedure. Completely different experiences. The difference wasn't luck. It was structural: one patient booked with a **facilitator**, and the other booked with a **broker**.

In [the first article of this series](/blog/how-to-choose-medical-tourism-partner-china), we gave you six questions to ask before committing to any medical tourism partner. This article goes one level deeper — into the business models behind the companies you'll encounter, and why the way a company earns its money quietly determines the advice you'll receive.

## The Three Types of Companies You'll Meet Online

Search "medical treatment in China" or "medical tourism agency" and you'll find dozens of companies with nearly identical websites: reassuring photos, lists of partner hospitals, promises of end-to-end service. Underneath the similar packaging, there are three fundamentally different business models.

### 1. The Travel Agent

A travel agency sells logistics: flights, hotels, airport transfers, maybe a hospital appointment bundled into a "medical travel package." Their expertise is tourism. They hold no medical knowledge, maintain no clinical relationships, and take no responsibility for what happens inside the hospital.

For a routine dental cleaning combined with a holiday, a travel agent might be adequate. For anything involving diagnosis, surgery, or staged treatment, they are structurally incapable of supporting you.

### 2. The Broker

A broker is an intermediary whose business is **introductions**. They connect you with a hospital, collect a referral fee, and step back. Some brokers are transparent about this. Many are not — their websites use the language of full service ("your personal medical coordinator," "24/7 support") while their actual operation ends the moment the hospital confirms your booking.

The broker's customer, in a very real sense, is not you. It's the hospital paying for patient volume.

### 3. The Facilitator

The international standard for medical tourism — ISO 22525, published by the International Organization for Standardization — defines a **facilitator** as a "medical-tourism-specialized intermediary which aids the medical tourist in the process of contracting medical services," often also arranging translation, transport, and accommodation.

In practice, a genuine facilitator manages your entire medical journey: reviewing your records before you fly, matching you to the right hospital and specialist, coordinating consultations, providing medical translation during treatment, and following your recovery for weeks or months after you return home.

The critical difference is accountability. A facilitator's engagement doesn't end at the hospital door — in a meaningful sense, that's where it begins.

## Follow the Money

If you remember one thing from this article, make it this: **ask how the company gets paid.**

Here's the number most patients never see. Referral commissions in medical tourism commonly run between **7.5% and 30% of the treatment bill**, paid by the hospital to the company that delivered the patient. On a $15,000 surgical package, that's $1,100 to $4,500 flowing back to whoever made the introduction — invisible to you, and built into the price you pay.

This commission structure explains a great deal of what goes wrong in medical tourism:

**It distorts hospital recommendations.** When one partner hospital pays 25% and another pays 10%, a commission-driven company has a financial reason to steer you toward the first — regardless of which is actually better for your case. You will never know whether the "perfect match" was clinical judgment or arithmetic.

**It discourages price transparency.** A broker earning a percentage of your bill benefits when your bill is higher. Itemized quotes, comparisons between hospitals, and honest conversations about cheaper adequate options all work against the commission model.

**It ends the relationship at the worst moment.** The broker earns its fee when you sign up with the hospital. Everything after that — your surgery, your complications, your recovery — costs the broker money to support and earns nothing. The incentives point exactly the wrong way.

### The Alternative: The Service-Fee Model

A facilitator working on a **service-fee model** charges you directly — a stated, transparent fee for coordination, translation, logistics, and advocacy. Your medical bills are paid to the hospital at the hospital's own rates.

Under this structure, the facilitator has only one customer: you. There is no second revenue stream pulling recommendations in a hidden direction. When such a facilitator suggests Hospital A over Hospital B, the only thing behind the suggestion is the case fit.

Neither model makes a company automatically good or bad. But only one of them lets you evaluate the advice you receive without wondering about the arithmetic behind it.

## What a Real Facilitator Actually Does

"End-to-end service" is easy to print on a website. Here's what it looks like in operational reality — the standard any company claiming the facilitator label should be able to demonstrate:

**Before you travel:**
- Collects and reviews your complete medical records, imaging, and history
- Arranges remote consultations with the treating hospital before you commit
- Provides a written, itemized treatment and cost estimate from the hospital itself
- Advises on visa category (for China, typically S1/S2 for medical purposes) and documentation
- Verifies whether your international insurer can be billed directly

**During your treatment:**
- Meets you on arrival and manages all ground logistics
- Provides professional medical translation — not a phone app — during consultations and consent discussions
- Coordinates between departments when your case crosses specialties
- Keeps your family informed if you wish
- Remains reachable at hours that match your anxiety, not their office schedule

**After you return home:**
- Coordinates follow-up consultations with the treating team
- Ensures your medical records, imaging, and discharge summaries are complete, in English, and in your hands
- Helps your home physician understand what was done and what to watch for
- Remains your channel back to the hospital if questions arise months later

If a company describes itself as a facilitator but cannot walk you through each of these stages concretely, you have learned something important.

## Seven Red Flags That You're Dealing with a Broker

None of these alone proves bad intent. Two or more together should change your decision.

1. **They recommend one specific hospital before reviewing your records.** A real matching process starts from your diagnosis, not from their partnership list.
2. **They can't or won't provide an itemized quote from the hospital.** Package pricing with no breakdown is how commission margins stay invisible.
3. **They discourage direct contact with the hospital.** "We'll handle all communication" often means "we don't want you comparing our price to the hospital's."
4. **Nobody on their team has a clinical background.** Ask who reviews your medical records and what qualifies them to do it.
5. **They have no physical presence in the destination country.** An operator coordinating Shanghai hospitals from another continent cannot meet you at arrivals or sit with you during a consent discussion.
6. **Communication quality drops after the deposit.** Test them before paying: ask a detailed question about post-operative follow-up and see how specific the answer is.
7. **The service agreement says nothing about post-treatment support.** What isn't written down will not happen.

## Four Questions That Reveal the Truth

Labels are unregulated — any broker can call itself a facilitator. These questions cut through the label to the structure underneath:

**"Are you paid by the hospital, by me, or both?"**
A transparent company answers this directly. Evasion here tells you everything.

**"Can I see the hospital's own price list for my procedure?"**
A facilitator on a service-fee model has no reason to hide it. A commission-driven broker does.

**"Who is my contact at 2 a.m. local time if something goes wrong after surgery — and what's their name?"**
Vague answers ("our support team") versus specific ones ("your coordinator, here's her number") reveal the difference between marketing language and operational reality.

**"What happens to your fee if I cancel after the first consultation?"**
A company confident in its service structure will have a clear, written answer.

## One More Structural Test: Insurance

Here's a detail that separates serious operators from introducers: **direct billing arrangements with international insurers.**

Major insurers covering treatment in China — Cigna, Allianz, AXA, Bupa among them — work through established billing relationships with hospital international departments. Coordinating direct billing requires the facilitator to understand both the insurer's pre-authorization requirements and the hospital's international patient processes, and to do the paperwork before treatment, not after.

Brokers rarely bother. It's labor-intensive, it earns them nothing extra, and it delays the booking that triggers their commission. A facilitator with genuine hospital relationships does it as a matter of course — because paying out of pocket and chasing reimbursement for months afterward is a burden you shouldn't have to carry.

When evaluating any company, ask: *"Can you arrange direct billing with my insurer, and which hospitals in your network support it?"* The specificity of the answer is diagnostic.

## An Honest Caveat

Fairness requires saying this: not every broker is a villain, and not every self-described facilitator delivers. Inbound medical travel in China is a young industry, and regulation has not caught up with marketing. There is currently no mandatory licensing regime that separates one model from the other — any company can use any label.

That's precisely why structural questions matter more than labels. Don't ask a company what it calls itself. Ask how it earns its money, who answers the phone after surgery, and what happens in month three of your recovery.

And it's also worth being honest about China's medical system itself: it is excellent in many dimensions — high specialist volumes, advanced technology, short waiting times — and genuinely difficult in others. Public hospitals are crowded. Privacy norms differ from what Western patients expect. Outside international departments, English is rare. A facilitator who tells you only the first half of that sentence is selling; one who tells you both halves is advising.
