## The Short Answer First

Yes, you can see a specialist in Shanghai as a foreigner — and in one way the system is simpler than what you're used to: **China has no GP gatekeeper.** You don't need a referral letter to see a cardiologist, an orthopedist, or a dermatologist. You book the specialist directly.

The hard parts are different ones: choosing the right department (Chinese hospitals divide specialties more finely than you might expect), navigating booking systems that are mostly in Chinese, and showing up with medical records a doctor can actually use.

I've spent over a decade coordinating international patient care in Shanghai. This is the exact process I walk patients through — six steps, plus the mistakes I see most often.

## Step 1: Work Out Which Specialty You Actually Need

This is where most foreign patients lose their first day. In a large Shanghai hospital, "back pain" could belong to spine surgery, orthopedics, pain management, rheumatology, or neurology — and registering for the wrong one means queuing, paying, and being told to start over somewhere else.

A few examples of how finely departments split:

- **Joint pain with morning stiffness** → rheumatology, not orthopedics
- **Back pain radiating down the leg** → spine surgery or orthopedics, depending on the hospital
- **Dizziness** → could be neurology or ENT, depending on the pattern
- **A lump or abnormal imaging** → the organ-specific surgical department, not general surgery

If you're not sure, don't guess. Write down your symptoms, duration, and previous diagnoses, and get an informed opinion on the right department *before* you book. (This is exactly what our MediMatch tool does — more on that below.)

## Step 2: Get Your Medical Records in Order

Specialists here work from documents. A folder with your imaging, lab results, and a one-page history is what turns a consultation into a real discussion of your case — instead of starting from "so, what brings you in?"

Prepare before you fly:

- **A concise medical summary** (ideally in Chinese): diagnoses, past medical history, allergies, the core clinical question, your current treatment plan, and other relevant medical history
- **Imaging reports**, ideally with the original imaging files (DICOM files on a CD or USB)
- **Lab results** — the more complete, the better; together they show how your condition has developed and help the doctor make judgments
- **Your medication list**

The full checklist is here: [Medical Records for Treatment in China: What to Prepare](/blog/medical-records-for-treatment-in-china-checklist).

## Step 3: Choose the Right Type of Hospital

For most foreign patients, there are two realistic routes:

| Route | Why choose it | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| **Public Grade 3A hospital, international department** | The best value for most patients: access to the country's top specialists at a lower cost than private care | Insurance supported; a calm, comfortable setting with attentive service — but not every staff member speaks English |
| **Private hospital or clinic** | Comfort and service: nicer facilities, more flexible scheduling, a more familiar experience | Insurance supported; higher prices |

Grade 3A (三甲) is the highest tier in China's official hospital grading system. **The international department route is the one we recommend**: you see the same leading specialists, in a setting built for international patients, at a price below private hospitals.

The right hospital is still the one whose specialty strength matches *your* condition — that varies case by case.

For a fuller comparison of hospital options, costs, and insurance, see [A Complete Guide for International Patients](/blog/medical-tourism-shanghai-complete-guide).

## Step 4: Book the Appointment

For international departments, booking is straightforward once you know the landscape:

- **By phone** — the main channel. Be prepared: operators are mostly Chinese-speaking, so have your key details written down (name as in your passport, department, preferred dates), or have a Chinese speaker help you.
- **WeChat mini-programs** — some hospitals support booking this way, but the schedule information shown is often incomplete, so don't assume a doctor is unavailable just because you can't see a slot.

Private hospitals also take phone bookings, and some accept appointments through their WeChat mini-programs — again, schedule information is often incomplete.

**Timing:** if you want a specific doctor, book **one to two weeks ahead**, whichever route you take. If you're flexible about which specialist you see, availability is generally comfortable — sometimes you can get an appointment as soon as the next day.

## Step 5: On the Day of Your Visit

Bring your passport, your records (with translations if you have them), and your imaging. The typical flow:

**Check in → wait → consultation → tests (if ordered) → payment → collect results.**

A few things worth knowing:

- **You get real consultation time.** In an international department, a specialist consultation typically runs around half an hour — as detailed and unhurried as a private-hospital visit. It still helps to arrive with your most important questions written down, so nothing gets forgotten.
- **Payment usually comes before the test, not after the visit.** You pay at a cashier window or machine, then take the receipt to the lab or imaging department. International departments support insurance billing; private hospitals do too.
- **Most tests can be done the same day**, and results come back fast. For the quickest items, you can view the report on your phone in about an hour.

If you don't speak Chinese, bring someone who does, or arrange interpretation in advance — don't assume English will be available everywhere. ## Step 6: After the Visit

Before you leave the hospital, make sure you have:

- **The written diagnosis and treatment plan**, and any prescriptions
- **Know how to view your reports and imaging on your phone** — some reports take one to three days, so remember to check back in time
- **Your medical records and itemized invoices** if you plan to claim on insurance
- **A clear follow-up plan**: when, with whom, and whether it can be done remotely

If your report is in Chinese and you're not fluent, get it properly translated before you act on it. This is not the place for guesswork or a phone camera translation of a pathology report.

## The Mistakes I See Most Often

1. **Registering for the wrong department** and losing a day to re-registration
2. **Assuming everyone in the international department speaks English** — many staff do, but don't count on every desk and every phone line; plan your language support in advance
3. **Not arranging interpretation for the consultation itself** — accurately understanding *and remembering* your diagnosis and treatment plan is the most important part of the visit
