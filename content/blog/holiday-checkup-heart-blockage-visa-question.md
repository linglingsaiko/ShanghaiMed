Some of the most serious findings in medical travel don't begin with a symptom. They begin with a routine checkup that a visitor booked almost as an afterthought.

That was the case for a retired teacher from Manchester in his late 50s, who came to Shanghai on the 30-day visa-free entry with his wife. The plan was the usual tourist itinerary — Yu Garden, the museums, soup dumplings — plus a full health checkup our team had booked at a public hospital's international department.

They landed on a Monday. Both checkups were on Wednesday.

Thursday afternoon his results came in. The international department arranged a same-day cardiac consultation, and the specialist pointed to the coronary CT angiogram (CTA): roughly 75% narrowing in his left anterior descending artery. Not an emergency — no chest pain, no immediate danger — but serious enough that it needed a stent, and serious enough that flying twelve hours home untreated wasn't something the cardiologist would endorse.

He was quiet walking back to the waiting area. He called his wife in from the hallway. Her first question: "Do we need to get home?"

A fair reaction — a cardiac diagnosis abroad makes anyone want their own doctors. But he asked us to walk them through the options first.

So we sat down with a coffee.

## Why treat it in Shanghai?

**1. The cardiology here is genuinely world-class.** A Grade 3A public hospital — China's highest tier — and the doctor on his case was a chief physician, the most senior clinical rank. Chinese interventional cardiologists are among the world's best, largely because of volume: the busiest centres run thousands of these procedures a year. This was not experimental or cut-rate care — it was top-tier.

**2. His insurance would cover it.** We checked with his insurer: the policy recognised this hospital and covered cardiac procedures here. He would pay first and claim reimbursement afterwards — we prepared all the invoices, reports and paperwork. No financial reason to rush home.

**3. Flying home meant waiting or paying.** On the NHS, an elective PCI after a finding like this can mean weeks on a list. Going private immediately meant paying £10,000–£18,000 out of pocket (2026 pricing). In Shanghai, insurance would reimburse the cost and the team could take him to theatre within days.

**4. Flying untreated wasn't a good idea.** The cardiologist was direct: a significant untreated blockage plus a long-haul flight — reduced cabin oxygen, limited backup — made treating it first the safer call. No one was stopping him from leaving.

The decision came quickly. They'd trusted this system for a checkup, and once the confusion cleared, trusting the same doctors for treatment was the obvious next step.

Then came the question every patient in this situation eventually asks:

"Wait — we came in visa-free for thirty days. If I need surgery, does our visa status change? Do we need to leave the country?"

## The question nobody plans for: the visa

Almost everyone gets confused here — this is one of the questions our team hears most often from visa-free visitors. The good news is straightforward:

**You do not need to leave China to sort out your stay status for medical reasons.**

The actual process:

- Once the treating team confirms the procedure, the hospital issues a formal diagnosis certificate and a proposed admission letter. You (or a family member, or your coordinator) take these to the local Exit-Entry Administration Bureau
- You apply for a **stay permit** — the document for visa-free entrants who need to remain beyond the visa-free period for legitimate reasons, medical treatment included
- Once accepted, the bureau issues a receipt valid for up to 7 working days; the decision must come within that window
- Your passport is typically held during processing — the receipt is your legal proof of stay. You are not "overstaying", and treatment continues as normal
- A spouse who needs to stay on to support you can apply through the same route with proof of relationship

Three practical points:

- You'll need to show you can cover treatment costs — hospital estimates plus proof of funds or insurance. His insurance confirmation was accepted
- The permit's duration follows your medical documentation — legally up to 180 days
- This is not a grey area. Article 34 of China's Exit and Entry Administration Law covers exactly this: visa-free entrants staying longer for legitimate reasons apply for a stay permit. Article 19 of the Regulations on Administration of the Exit-Entry of Foreign Nationals (revised 2025) allows family or service agencies to apply on behalf of hospitalised patients

## How it played out

A coronary angiogram on Friday confirmed the narrowing; the stent went in on Monday morning, four days after diagnosis. Under an hour on the table. Two nights in the international ward; his wife stayed at a hotel nearby.

The morning after, he was sitting up in the ward lounge, flipping through a guidebook. The ward had him on a carefully managed heart-healthy diet. He joked he felt "ridiculously normal" for someone who'd had a cardiac procedure the day before. His wife's comment was simpler: "The private room is nicer than our local hospital back home."

We submitted his stay-permit application the day after admission. By discharge he had the official receipt — fully legal while processed. The permit came through a few days later.

They stayed another three weeks — slow daily walks along the Bund, cafés, and a high-speed train day trip to Suzhou when he felt up to it. By the second week he was exploring at his own pace.

They flew home just over four weeks after landing. His cardiologist in Manchester reviewed the stent images and follow-up notes: result good, continue dual antiplatelet therapy, and check in when settled.

## Three honest notes

- This is not a recommendation to arrive visa-free and plan surgery. If you already know you need a procedure, apply for the right visa from home. The stay-permit route exists for things that come up after arrival
- If your permitted stay is running short, start at least 7 days before expiry. China's multilingual immigration hotline: +86-12367
- Visa-free entry is fine for checkups, dental work and outpatient visits — not for planned admission. Every case differs; confirm with the hospital and the local bureau
