Every week I get messages from overseas patients asking the same question in different words:

> "I found a famous TCM doctor online who says he can cure my [condition]. Should I book a flight to China?"

My answer is always the same: not every "famous TCM doctor" is worth your flight — and not every "traditional master" is legitimate. But the picture isn't as simple as "hospital = safe, folk practitioner = dangerous."

After over a decade coordinating international patient care in Shanghai, here is what I tell every friend and client who asks.

## The good news: there are two legal paths, not one

A common misconception among foreign patients is that a TCM doctor is either "hospital-trained and real" or "self-taught and fake." That misses how Chinese medicine actually works in modern China.

Under Chinese law, there are **two fully legitimate pathways** to becoming a licensed TCM physician:

**Path 1: The university-and-hospital route.** A physician completes a five- or six-year degree in Traditional Chinese Medicine at a medical university (such as Shanghai University of TCM, Beijing University of Chinese Medicine, or Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine), passes the national medical licensing examination, completes residency, and builds a career inside the public hospital system — rising through the ranks from resident to attending to associate chief and finally chief physician (主任医师). This is the path most patients from outside China intuitively understand, and it produces the majority of senior TCM doctors working at Grade 3A (三甲) hospitals today.

**Path 2: The master-apprentice and recognised-expertise route.** For thousands of years, Chinese medicine was transmitted through long-term apprenticeship, not university classrooms. Modern Chinese law recognises this. Under Article 15 of the 2017 *Law on Traditional Chinese Medicine* and the 2017 assessment and registration measures, an applicant learning through apprenticeship must document **five continuous years** of study with a qualified TCM master and obtain recommendations from at least two licensed TCM physicians (not including the master). A separate provision covers practitioners with recognised expertise gained through years of eligible practice. Applicants take a provincial-level practical-skills and outcomes assessment. Those who pass receive a TCM (Special Expertise) physician qualification and must register within the assessed scope of practice — this is not an unrestricted licence to treat every condition. As of the end of 2023, roughly 13,000 practitioners nationally had obtained their licenses through this "special expertise" (确有专长) route, according to a 2025 response from the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine to a National People's Congress representative.

Both paths are legal. Both require a license. Both can produce excellent doctors. And — this is the critical part — neither path guarantees excellence on its own. A chief physician at a top hospital may be burnt out and rushed; a respected apprentice-trained physician may have a decades-long track record of helping patients that the system took a long time to formally recognise.

So how do you actually choose?

## Four checks that matter more than the title

Forget the marketing. Forget the viral articles. These four checks filter out most of the risk.

### 1. Verify the medical license

This is non-negotiable. Every TCM physician legally practising in China holds either:

- A **TCM Physician Qualification Certificate** (中医医师资格证) issued by the National Health Commission after passing the national exam (Path 1), or
- A **TCM (Special Expertise) Physician Qualification Certificate** (中医(专长)医师资格证) issued by the provincial TCM authority after passing the practice-skills assessment (Path 2).

Ask for the name on the license and cross-check it. For public hospitals, the physician's name, title, and department are listed on the hospital's official website. For licensed independent clinics, the practicing physician's registration details are filed with the local health commission and should be visible on the clinic's practice license (医疗机构执业许可证), which is required by law to be displayed on the premises.

If someone cannot or will not show you a verifiable license, that is not a "hidden master." That is an unlicensed practitioner. Walk away.

### 2. Look at long-term patient results, not testimonials

A glowing social-media post from one patient who "felt amazing after one session" is not evidence. Neither is a wall of celebrity photos.

What you want to know is:

- **How long has this person been practising?** Five years is a minimum benchmark. Twenty or thirty years at the same craft, in the same city, is a strong signal.
- **What do patients with your specific condition say after weeks or months, not days?** TCM works cumulatively, especially for chronic issues. A practitioner who reliably helps a specific group of patients (migraine, chronic back pain, insomnia, digestive issues) is more trustworthy than one who claims equal mastery over everything.
- **Are there patients who returned multiple times?** Repeat consultations over months and years, from patients who could have gone anywhere, are the most honest endorsement.

### 3. Notice what happens during the first consultation

A good TCM physician — regardless of which path they came through — will spend time on you. Expect:

- 15–30 minutes of questions about your symptoms, sleep, appetite, digestion, energy, emotional state, and medical history;
- Reading of your tongue and pulse (this is part of the standard four examinations: 望闻问切);
- Review of any Western medical records, imaging, or lab results you bring — and a willingness to say "you should get this checked by a Western doctor first" if red-flag symptoms appear;
- A clear explanation of the pattern they identify and the treatment plan.

If a practitioner glances at you for thirty seconds, writes a prescription without asking questions, or discourages you from getting Western diagnostic tests, that is a problem regardless of how many framed photos are on the wall.

### 4. Ask what they *don't* treat

The most honest signal a doctor can give is knowing their own limits.

A good TCM physician will say: "This is outside my scope — you need to see a specialist." Or: "For this condition, TCM can help manage symptoms alongside your Western treatment, not replace it." Or: "Let's try 4–6 sessions and reassess; if you're not improving, we need to reconsider."

If they never refer out, never recommend a Western workup, and never acknowledge a limitation, that is a sign they are selling certainty rather than practising medicine.

## Our recommendation for international patients

For patients travelling from abroad — different healthcare culture, limited Mandarin, time constraints — we consistently recommend senior physicians (Chief Physicians or Associate Chief Physicians) at top Grade 3A public hospitals' TCM departments, or at well-established TCM hospitals with international patient services.

The reasons are practical:

- **Credentials are verifiable** on the hospital's official site;
- **They read MRI and lab reports** as a matter of routine and will not dismiss Western diagnostic findings;
- **They are accountable** inside a regulated hospital system;
- **The cost is transparent** — a Chief Physician's specialist consultation at a Shanghai public hospital's TCM department typically runs 100–500 RMB (roughly $14–$70); an acupuncture session at a public hospital international clinic or special-needs clinic typically runs 200–500 RMB ($28–$70), while standard public hospital sessions start lower;
- **They will refer to other departments** if your condition turns out to need Western intervention.

This is not because master-apprentice-trained physicians are inferior. It is because verifying an apprentice-trained physician's track record from overseas, without local language skills and local networks, is genuinely harder. For someone spending thousands of dollars on a flight and lodging, reducing verification risk matters.

There are outstanding physicians from both paths. If you live in China, have strong personal referrals from local patients you trust, and can verify the specialty license, a master-apprentice-trained physician can absolutely be the right choice.

## The one red flag that always means walk away

It does not matter how famous they are. It does not matter how many WeChat articles they've been featured in. It does not matter which path they took.

**If anyone promises to cure everything — "包治百病" in Chinese — leave.**

- If they guarantee a cure for cancer, diabetes, autoimmune disease, infertility, or any other serious condition before even examining you;
- If they tell you to stop your Western medication and use their herbs instead;
- If they demand large upfront payments for "complete treatment courses" with no follow-up mechanism;
- If they discourage you from getting a second opinion or from seeing a Western doctor;

… that is not a master healer. That is a salesperson.

A good TCM physician, regardless of lineage or title, does the opposite: they ask questions, they examine carefully, they acknowledge limits, they make measured claims, they treat you as a whole person — and they never promise a miracle.
