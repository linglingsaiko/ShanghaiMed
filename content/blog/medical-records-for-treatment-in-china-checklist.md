Before you book a flight for treatment in China, organize the records that explain your current medical problem clearly. The goal is not to send every document you have ever received. It is to help the reviewing doctor understand what has happened, what has already been tested or tried, what you take now, and what you want to ask.

A well-prepared record set can make that review more efficient. It cannot guarantee a diagnosis before you arrive, prevent repeat tests, or replace an in-person examination. The receiving doctor decides what can be accepted and what needs to be checked again.

Use this checklist whether you are planning treatment in Shanghai, asking for a second opinion, or helping a family member prepare for a hospital visit.

In my coordination work, I start with the decision the patient is facing—not the number of files. If the question is whether a proposed operation deserves a second opinion, the operation note, imaging and pathology may need priority. For a first consultation about an ongoing symptom, the symptom timeline and previous treatment may be just as important. The order of the file should follow the clinical question, not an arbitrary rule to send everything.

## The short version: your pre-travel records checklist

Start with the records related to your current concern, then add the background information that could affect safe care.

| Prepare | What to include |
|---|---|
| A short medical summary | Your current concern, relevant diagnoses, major past treatment, current doctors and what you want from the visit |
| Relevant test reports | Recent laboratory, imaging, cardiac, pathology or other reports related to the problem |
| Original scan images, when available | The image files as well as the radiology report; keep the examination date and facility details attached |
| Previous treatment records | Consultation and discharge summaries, operation notes, treatment plans and follow-up recommendations |
| A complete medication list | Prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, vitamins, herbal products and supplements, with doses and actual use |
| Allergies and previous reactions | The substance, what happened and whether it was an allergy, intolerance or another adverse effect, if known |
| Relevant medical history | Chronic conditions, previous hospitalizations, operations and other information that may affect care |
| A symptom timeline and priority questions | When symptoms began, how they changed, what affects them and what you most want the doctor to explain |

Keep older or less relevant records in a separate archive. Do not delete them: an old result may provide a useful baseline or show how a condition has changed. The point is to put the most useful information first, not to erase the past.

## 1. Start with a short medical summary

A doctor reviewing records from another country may be working through different formats, languages and naming conventions. A short summary gives them a way into the file before they read the original reports.

Include:

- **Your identifying details:** your name as shown in your passport and your date of birth. Share these only through an agreed, appropriate channel.
- **The main reason for this consultation:** describe the problem in plain language rather than assigning yourself a new diagnosis.
- **Diagnoses already given by a clinician:** where useful, distinguish a confirmed diagnosis from a suspected one or a finding still being investigated.
- **Relevant past conditions and treatment:** focus on information that could change the doctor's assessment or treatment choices.
- **Your current clinicians:** include the doctors or facilities involved in the current problem, especially if records may need to be requested or clarified later.
- **Your goal for the visit:** for example, understanding a diagnosis, reviewing a proposed operation, discussing treatment options or planning ongoing care.

This summary is a guide to the records, not a replacement for them. Keep the original reports so the doctor can check the wording, measurements, dates and clinical context themselves.

## 2. Select reports related to the current problem

A large unsorted folder forces the reviewer to work out which documents matter before they can begin assessing your case. Instead, place the current problem first and arrange the supporting evidence around it.

Depending on your situation, that may include:

- recent laboratory results;
- imaging reports such as CT, MRI, ultrasound or X-ray reports;
- electrocardiograms or other diagnostic test reports;
- consultation notes from the clinicians managing the problem;
- discharge summaries from related hospital admissions;
- operation or procedure notes;
- pathology reports;
- previous treatment plans and records of how you responded; and
- follow-up recommendations that have not yet been completed.

There is no universal rule that only records from a fixed number of months matter. A recent result may show the current situation, while an older result may be important for comparison. If you are unsure whether an older document is relevant, keep it in the archive and ask the reviewing clinician or coordination team rather than discarding it.

Also check that each document is complete. A cropped screenshot without the examination date, patient details, units or final page may be difficult to interpret safely. Clear copies of complete documents are more useful than a large collection of partial images.

## 3. Send the imaging report and the original images

A radiology report describes the radiologist's interpretation. It is not the same thing as the scan images themselves. When original images are available, ask the issuing facility for a copy in its standard electronic format, often DICOM, and keep the accompanying report with them. In the United States, patients have a formal right to request copies of their health information, including medical images; procedures differ by country and facility, so confirm the issuing provider's process early [5].

Before sending imaging:

- confirm that the files belong to the correct person and examination;
- keep the examination date and facility information;
- include all relevant sequences or files supplied by the facility;
- avoid relying on photographs of a computer screen when the original files can be obtained; and
- ask the receiving hospital which format and transfer method it can accept.

You do not need to interpret the images yourself. You also should not assume that supplying them will automatically prevent another scan. Image quality, clinical changes, technical requirements and the question the receiving doctor needs to answer can all affect whether new imaging is recommended.

For a second opinion, the US National Cancer Institute explains that another specialist may review materials related to the case, including test results and X-rays [3]. That principle is useful beyond cancer care, but the exact records required still depend on the specialty and the clinical question.

## 4. Include treatment records—not only test results

Test results show what was found. Treatment records explain what was done next and what happened afterward. Both can matter.

If relevant to your current problem, gather:

- operation and procedure notes;
- anesthesia records when specifically requested;
- discharge summaries;
- pathology reports;
- treatment plans and medication changes;
- rehabilitation or follow-up instructions;
- information about implanted devices or prostheses, where applicable; and
- records of complications, side effects or reasons a treatment was stopped.

Pathology needs special handling in some cases. The National Cancer Institute notes that a pathology second opinion may require slides and/or paraffin blocks, not just the written report [4]. These materials are not necessary for every patient. If your case involves pathology, ask the receiving institution whether it offers review, exactly what it needs, how materials should be packaged and transported, whether originals can be returned, and what fees may apply. Do not ship irreplaceable slides or blocks without confirmed instructions.

## 5. Make the medication list reflect what you actually take

A medication list should cover more than prescriptions. Mayo Clinic's medical-travel checklist includes prescription medicines, over-the-counter products and supplements, with doses and the reason for taking them [1]. MSD Manual also specifically includes medicinal herbs and vitamins in the information patients should prepare [2].

For each item, record:

- the brand name and generic name, if known;
- the strength and dose;
- how often and by which route you take it;
- why you take it;
- whether you use it regularly or only when needed; and
- any side effects or problems you have experienced.

Include vitamins, herbal products, traditional medicines, sleep aids, pain relievers, creams, injections and products bought without a prescription when relevant. If your actual use differs from the instructions on the label, record what you actually do and mention the difference to the doctor. Do not hide missed doses or products you think may be unimportant.

A photograph of the original packaging can help clarify a product name, but it should support—not replace—a readable list. Do not stop, start or change a medicine because you are preparing for a trip. Any change belongs with a qualified clinician who knows your situation. Rules for carrying medicines across borders are a separate issue and should be checked for your specific products and itinerary.

### Separate allergies from side effects when you can

“Penicillin caused nausea” and “penicillin caused swelling and breathing difficulty” communicate very different histories. For each allergy or previous reaction, list the substance and what happened. If you do not know whether it was an allergy, intolerance or another adverse effect, say that honestly rather than guessing. Include reactions to medicines, contrast agents, foods or other substances when they are relevant to your care.

## 6. Write a symptom timeline in plain language

Symptoms are often harder to reconstruct during a short appointment, especially when an interpreter is involved. MSD Manual recommends describing frequency, duration, and factors that make symptoms better or worse, and preparing questions before the visit [2].

A useful timeline answers:

- When did the problem begin?
- Was the onset sudden or gradual?
- What has changed since then?
- How often does it happen, and how long does an episode last?
- What makes it better or worse?
- What other symptoms occur at the same time?
- How does it affect sleep, movement, eating, work or daily activities?
- What treatment have you already tried, and what happened?

Use your own words. You do not need to turn the timeline into a self-diagnosis, and you should not minimize or exaggerate symptoms to make the story sound more persuasive. A short factual account is more useful.

### Put your most important questions first

Write down what you want to understand before the appointment. If the list becomes long, choose your top three and put them first. Useful questions often include:

- What is the current working diagnosis, and what remains uncertain?
- Which existing records are useful, and what is still missing?
- Would another test change the decision, and what would it add?
- What are the reasonable treatment options, their goals and their main risks?
- What needs to happen before travel, and what should wait until after an examination?
- What records and follow-up will my local doctor need afterward?

The right questions depend on your case. The purpose is to leave the consultation with clearer decisions, not to force the doctor into a predetermined answer.

## 7. Organize files so another clinician can navigate them

Good organization is part of clinical communication. A simple structure is enough:

```text
00_Medical-summary-and-questions
01_Current-test-reports
02_Imaging-reports-and-original-images
03_Treatment-and-hospital-records
04_Medication-allergy-and-history-lists
05_Older-records-archive
```

Use clear filenames that include the examination date and document type where possible, such as `YYYY-MM-DD_MRI-report_facility`. Keep the original document and its translation together, but do not overwrite the original. Check that scanned pages are upright, complete and readable before sending them.

### Translate the key documents, not every page by default

Ask the receiving clinician or coordinator which documents need translation and into which language. The current summary, medication and allergy lists, key diagnostic reports, operation notes and pathology reports are often higher priorities than a large archive of unrelated documents.

Medical translation needs particular care with medicine names, doses, units, negations, allergy descriptions and diagnostic wording. Keep uncertain terms marked for review rather than “smoothing” them into something that sounds clearer but changes the meaning. Machine translation may help you understand a document privately, but it should not be treated as a verified clinical translation.

### Protect privacy and confirm consent

Medical records contain sensitive information. Confirm the recipient and use the transfer method agreed with the hospital or coordination provider. Do not post identifiable reports in public groups or include unrelated people's details unnecessarily.

A family member can help collect and organize documents, but an adult patient's permission should be obtained before their records are shared. Family assistance does not automatically replace the patient's own authorization or decisions about care.

## 8. What preparation cannot do

Being organized is valuable, but it is important not to oversell what records can achieve.

**Complete records do not guarantee that no tests will be repeated.** The receiving doctor may need a current result, a different technique, or an examination performed to a particular clinical standard.

**A remote document review is not automatically a diagnosis or acceptance for treatment.** Ask what the review means, what remains provisional and whether an in-person assessment is required.

**More pages do not necessarily mean a better review.** Relevant, readable and well-labelled information is more useful than an unfiltered archive.

**Travel preparation should not delay urgent care.** New or severe symptoms such as acute chest pain, possible stroke signs or severe breathing difficulty require prompt local emergency assessment. Do not wait for a planned consultation in Shanghai. This list is not a complete guide to emergency symptoms.

## 9. Final check before you send anything

Before sharing your file, check:

- [ ] Your name and date of birth are consistent across the key documents.
- [ ] The current concern and medical summary appear first.
- [ ] Reports are complete, readable and arranged in a clear order.
- [ ] Original images and their corresponding reports are included when available and requested.
- [ ] Medication, supplement and allergy lists reflect your actual situation.
- [ ] Original documents remain unchanged alongside any translations.
- [ ] Your symptom timeline and highest-priority questions are included.
- [ ] Older records are separated but preserved.
- [ ] The recipient, purpose and transfer method have been confirmed.
- [ ] The patient has authorized any family member or coordinator involved in sharing the records.

It is also reasonable to ask the recipient: Which specialty should review this case? Are any key records missing? Are original images required? Which documents need professional translation? Does this review amount only to preliminary guidance, or has a clinician formally assessed the case?

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I bring every medical record I have?

Keep your complete history available, but lead with records related to the current concern and background that could affect safe care. Ask the receiving clinician which older records are useful for comparison rather than sending one unsorted archive.

### Are photographs of reports enough?

Clear photographs may help identify a document, but complete, readable copies are preferable for clinical review. For imaging, the written report and original image files serve different purposes; ask whether DICOM or another original format is required.

### Do all documents need to be translated into Chinese?

Not necessarily. Requirements vary by hospital and clinician. Confirm the required language and prioritize the summary, medication and allergy lists, key diagnostic reports and treatment records. Keep translations linked to unchanged originals.

### Will complete records help me avoid repeat tests?

Not always. Good records may help the doctor understand what has already been done, but repeat testing can still be necessary because of clinical changes, quality or technical requirements, or a different question being investigated.

### Can a family member organize and send my records?

A family member can help, but an adult patient's authorization should be obtained before records are shared. The patient should remain involved in decisions about their own care.
