Is Shanghai Safe for Medical Treatment? A Guide for International Patients
Shanghai has quietly become one of the most sought-after destinations for international medical care in Asia. If you're considering coming here for treatment—whether it's a routine health checkup, dental work, or something more complex—you probably have questions. Is the care good? Will I be understood? How much will it cost? Can I use my insurance?
This guide answers all of those questions and more. Think of it as advice from a friend who knows the Shanghai healthcare system inside and out.
Why Patients Are Choosing Shanghai
Let's start with the numbers, because they tell a compelling story.
Shanghai's public hospitals now receive 73,000+ international patient visits per year, according to the Shanghai Municipal Health Commission (2025). That's not a small pilot program—that's a well-established, growing system designed to serve people from around the world.
Here's what that looks like in practice:
23 public hospitals now operate dedicated international medical services (Source: Shanghai International Service Portal)
International medical centers serve patients from 90+ nationalities (Source: Huashan Hospital International Medical Center, 2025)
The number of international patients has grown by 25% year-over-year (Source: Shanghai Municipal Health Commission, 2024)
Why are so many people choosing Shanghai? A few reasons stand out:
Globally competitive medical expertise. Shanghai's physicians stand on par with the best in the world—many serve on international medical committees, publish in leading global journals, and collaborate with peers across continents. Their expertise is built on China's rigorous medical training and the sheer volume of cases only a population this large can provide, further enriched by active international exchange and collaboration.
Cutting-edge technology. Shanghai's major hospitals invest heavily in advanced diagnostic and treatment equipment—da Vinci surgical systems, PET-MRI scanners, proton therapy, and more.
Significant cost savings. Even with travel expenses factored in, treatment in Shanghai typically costs 40-70% less than equivalent care in the US or Western Europe.
Geographic convenience. As a major international hub, Shanghai is easily accessible from most Asian cities, and visa policies have become increasingly foreigner-friendly.
Public vs. International Hospitals: What's the Difference?
A critical difference from the West: If you're from the US or Europe, you probably assume private hospitals deliver the best care. In China, that's not the case. China's top-tier medical institutions are public Grade 3A (三甲) hospitals—the country's most experienced specialists, most advanced equipment, and deepest expertise in complex cases are concentrated there. Think of it this way: in the US, the best hospitals are private (Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic); in China, the best hospitals are public. This is a fundamental difference that shapes the entire healthcare landscape, and understanding it will help you make better decisions about your care.
Shanghai offers two main pathways for international patients: public hospital international departments and private international hospitals. Each has distinct advantages.
Public Hospital International Departments
Advantages:
Access to China's top specialists and subspecialists—these are the physicians other doctors refer their most complex cases to
The most advanced medical equipment in the country, backed by substantial government investment (PET-MRI, da Vinci surgical systems, proton therapy, and other cutting-edge technology are typically deployed at major public hospitals first)
Affiliated with leading academic medical centers with extensive research capabilities
More affordable than private international hospitals
Comprehensive diagnostic capabilities with on-site laboratories and multi-disciplinary specialist teams
Considerations:
Busier environment, though international departments have dedicated staff and separate waiting areas
English-speaking coordinators are available in international departments, but the broader hospital environment operates primarily in Chinese
Best for: Complex diagnoses, surgeries, treatments requiring top subspecialists, and cases where medical expertise is the primary concern.
Private International Hospitals
Advantages:
Premium hotel-like environment with private rooms
Full English-speaking environment throughout the facility—from reception to nursing staff to physicians
Longer consultation times with more detailed, patient-centered communication
Appointments available quickly for most services
Comprehensive services under one roof (consultations, diagnostics, pharmacy, dental)
Considerations:
Higher costs than public hospital international departments
For highly complex cases, specialists may refer to public hospital colleagues
Some insurance companies classify certain private hospitals as High Cost Providers (HCP) or "expensive medical facilities" (昂贵医疗). Depending on your insurance plan, visits to HCP-listed hospitals may require a 20% copay—while the same treatment at non-HCP facilities would be covered in full. This is worth checking before you commit to a facility
Best for: Preventive care, routine checkups, minor procedures, dental work, pediatrics, and patients who prioritize comfort and communication.
Many international patients use a combination of both: complex treatment at public hospital international departments where top specialists and advanced equipment are concentrated, routine follow-ups and preventive care at private international clinics where the experience is more comfortable.

English-Speaking Services: Will You Be Understood?
This is often the biggest concern for international patients, and understandably so. The good news: Shanghai's international healthcare system has invested heavily in communication infrastructure.
Dedicated international departments at major public hospitals employ bilingual coordinators who assist with everything from appointment booking to explaining treatment plans. These coordinators often have medical backgrounds or specialized training. While the coordinators bridge the language gap effectively, the overall English environment in public hospitals is not as seamless as in private facilities—the broader hospital operates primarily in Chinese, and you may encounter staff outside the international department who speak limited English.
Private international hospitals offer a fully English-speaking experience—reception, nursing staff, physicians, and administrative teams all communicate in English. Consultations tend to be longer and more detailed, with physicians taking extra time to explain conditions and treatment options.
Insurance Acceptance: Can You Use Your Plan?
Many international patients wonder whether their health insurance will work in Shanghai. The answer is increasingly yes.
Direct billing is available at both public hospital international departments and private international hospitals in Shanghai—the process is equally smooth at both types of facilities. The hospital bills your insurance company directly; you pay only what your plan doesn't cover. Private international hospitals currently have the most extensive direct billing networks, though major public hospital international departments are rapidly expanding their insurance partnerships.
Watch out for the "expensive medical facility" classification. Some private hospitals are categorized by insurers as High Cost Providers (HCPs) or "expensive medical facilities" (昂贵医疗). If your insurance plan includes HCP coverage, treatment at these facilities is typically covered in full. However, many plans offer a lower-premium option with a 20% copay at HCPs—meaning you pay 20% out-of-pocket at these facilities while receiving 100% coverage elsewhere. This is the most popular plan choice among expats in China (84% opt for some form of HCP copay to reduce premiums). Check your specific plan details before committing to a facility.

Making Payments in Shanghai
Paying for healthcare—and everything else—in China has become dramatically easier for international visitors. If you visited even a few years ago and struggled with cash-only situations, you'll find a very different landscape today.
Mobile payments now accept foreign cards. Both Alipay and WeChat Pay allow you to bind international Visa, Mastercard, and other foreign credit/debit cards. Once linked, you can pay for medical services, pharmacy purchases, transportation, and daily expenses just like a local—with a few taps on your phone. The setup takes about 5 minutes and requires just your passport and card details.
36 overseas e-wallets work directly in China. Through the People's Bank of China's cross-border payment integration, e-wallets from countries including Malaysia (Touch'n Go), South Korea (Kakao Pay), Hong Kong (AlipayHK), Thailand (TrueMoney), and many others can be used at merchants across Shanghai without needing a separate Chinese payment account.
PayPal and WeChat Pay integration. PayPal has partnered with WeChat Pay, allowing PayPal users to make payments through the WeChat ecosystem—another convenient bridge for visitors who prefer not to set up local payment methods.
Small transactions are fee-free. For transactions under ¥200 (approximately $28), no additional currency conversion fees are charged—making pharmacy runs, meals, and transportation payments hassle-free.
Practical tips for medical payments:
Set up Alipay or WeChat Pay with your foreign card before arriving—you'll use it for everything from taxis to hospital copays
Large hospitals accept international credit cards at their international departments
Keep digital receipts for insurance claims
Most international clinics provide itemized invoices in English for reimbursement purposes
Travel and Visa Considerations
Getting to Shanghai and navigating entry requirements is simpler than you might expect.
The 144-Hour Visa-Free Transit is a game-changer for many international patients. If you're from one of 50+ eligible countries (including the US, UK, Canada, Australia, Japan, and most EU nations), you can transit through Shanghai without a visa for up to 144 hours (6 days). This is particularly useful for:
Quick consultations and minor procedures
Health checkups that don't require extended stays
Follow-up visits for patients who received initial treatment elsewhere
Medical Visas (M Visa) are appropriate for patients requiring longer treatment periods. You'll need:
Invitation letter from your Shanghai hospital or clinic
Proof of financial means or insurance
Valid passport (6+ months validity recommended)
Completed visa application
Getting to Shanghai is convenient—Pudong International Airport (PVG) and Hongqiao International Airport (SHA) connect to most major cities worldwide. Many international patients fly in, have their appointment, and fly out the same or next day for minor procedures, or arrange accommodation for longer treatment plans.
Accommodation near major hospitals ranges from budget options to luxury hotels and serviced apartments. International hospitals often have partnerships with nearby accommodations for patient families.
